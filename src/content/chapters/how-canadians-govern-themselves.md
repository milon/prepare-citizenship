Canada is a **[constitutional monarchy](/glossary/#constitutional-monarchy)** and a **[parliamentary democracy](/glossary/#parliamentary-democracy)** inside a **[federation](/glossary/#federalism)**. Three labels, three ideas.

## Head of State and Head of Government

- The **Monarch** is **Head of State**.
- The **[Governor General](/glossary/#governor-general)** represents the Monarch in Canada: grants Royal Assent, reads the Speech from the Throne, and carries out ceremonial and constitutional duties on advice.
- The **[Prime Minister](/glossary/#prime-minister)** is **Head of Government**, leader of the party that can command the House of Commons.
- The **party in power** is the party that can command confidence in the House.

Names of office-holders change. The offices do not. Current names are listed on this page from a dated file so questions do not go stale overnight.

Each province has a **[Lieutenant Governor](/glossary/#lieutenant-governor)** representing the [Crown](/glossary/#crown). Territories have commissioners.

## Three parts of Parliament

Federal Parliament has:

1. The **[Crown](/glossary/#crown)**
2. The **[Senate](/glossary/#senate)** — appointed on the Prime Minister’s advice; reviews legislation
3. The **[House of Commons](/glossary/#commons)** — elected [Members of Parliament (MPs)](/glossary/#mp)

A bill usually needs to pass the Commons and the Senate and then receive **[Royal Assent](/glossary/#royal-assent)**. Confidence votes happen in the Commons: if the government loses confidence, it must resign or call an election.

## Federalism

The Constitution divides powers. In broad strokes:

- **Federal**: defence, criminal law, money and banking, foreign affairs, citizenship, trade between provinces and with other countries
- **Provincial**: education, health care delivery, property and civil rights, municipalities
- **Municipal**: local matters delegated by the province (transit, snow clearing, zoning)
- **Territorial**: similar day-to-day responsibilities, with more federal involvement in their constitutional setup

**Indigenous governments** also exercise jurisdiction under treaties, statutes, and self-government agreements.

## How government is chosen

After an election, the Governor General asks the leader most likely to hold the confidence of the House to form a government. That leader becomes Prime Minister and names **[Cabinet](/glossary/#cabinet)**. Cabinet ministers are usually MPs (sometimes senators). Cabinet solidarity and secrecy are conventions: ministers defend government decisions in public.

**Opposition** parties hold the government to account. The **Leader of the Opposition** leads the largest party not in government. The **[Speaker](/glossary/#speaker)** chairs debates in the Commons and is expected to be impartial.

## The Constitution

Key written parts include the Constitution Act, 1867 (originally the British North America Act) and the Constitution Act, 1982 (including the Charter). Unwritten **conventions** also matter: responsible government is mostly convention, not a single statute.
