Canada has a **mixed economy**: private businesses and markets produce most goods and services, while governments tax, regulate, provide public services, and sometimes own Crown corporations.

## What the country sells

Natural resources still matter: **energy** (oil, gas, hydroelectricity), **minerals**, **forest products**, and **grain**. Canada is a large agricultural exporter. **Manufacturing**—vehicles, machinery, food processing, aerospace—clusters in central Canada and in other industrial cities. **Services** (banking, insurance, real estate, education, health, retail, technology) employ most people.

The **United States** is by far the largest trading partner. Supply chains cross the border many times on a single product. Canada also trades with Europe, China, and other partners, and belongs to the **G7** group of advanced economies.

Historians call the older pattern the **staples** economy: exporting raw or lightly processed goods—fur, then timber, wheat, and minerals—rather than finished products. Traces of it remain, but today's exports also include vehicles, aircraft, and services.

## Trade and its frictions

Free-trade agreements with the United States and Mexico, and later with the European Union and Pacific partners, matter most to **exporters and manufacturers** who need predictable access to bigger markets. Auto manufacturing in particular depends on integrated, cross-border supply chains and tariff-free parts.

Open trade does not mean the absence of disputes. **Softwood lumber** has been a recurring Canada–U.S. quarrel for decades, because the two countries disagree about whether Canadian timber pricing amounts to a subsidy. **Interprovincial** trade has frictions of its own: provinces regulate some goods and professional credentials differently, so selling or working across a provincial boundary is not always as simple as it sounds.

Ports such as **Vancouver** and **Halifax** matter because a trading country needs saltwater access on both coasts. Tourism counts as an export in economic terms: when visitors spend money here, Canada earns foreign income without shipping anything.

## Regional economies

- **Atlantic**: fisheries, energy, tourism, and public services; some areas have had slower growth and out-migration
- **Central Canada**: the largest share of population and manufacturing; Toronto as a financial centre; Montreal as a diversified metropolis
- **Prairies**: grain, potash, oil and gas
- **British Columbia**: forestry, mining, film, ports, and Pacific trade
- **North**: mining, public administration, and high costs of living and transport

## Work, unions, and the public sector

Most workers are in the private sector, but government employment is significant, especially outside large cities. Unions are legal; collective bargaining is part of labour law. Employment standards (minimum wage, hours, parental leave) are mainly provincial.

**Crown corporations** are companies owned by a government to deliver a public service or fill a strategic role—broadcasting, transport, and utilities are common examples. They operate as businesses but answer to the government that owns them.

## Taxes and social programs

Income taxes, sales taxes, and other revenues pay for health care, defence, roads, schools, and the rest of public spending. Personal income tax is **progressive**: rates rise in brackets as income rises, so higher earners pay a larger share of each additional dollar. Filing a return every year is a normal obligation of living here.

**Employment Insurance** and public pensions exist to cushion predictable risks—losing a job, or reaching an age where work is no longer possible. They are funded through contributions and taxes rather than private savings alone.

All three levels of government spend in the economy, not just the federal one. Provinces run health and education, by far the biggest budget items in the country, and municipalities handle local services.

## Money and banks

The **Bank of Canada** is the central bank. It sets monetary policy and issues bank notes. Commercial banks are large, privately owned, and heavily regulated—so Canada has both a public central bank and a private banking industry. Canada's dollar **floats** on foreign-exchange markets, meaning its value moves with supply and demand rather than being pegged.

A historical note that still appears in questions: the **Canadian Wheat Board** once acted as the single marketing agency for Prairie wheat and barley exports, giving farmers collective bargaining power over price. That monopoly role has since ended.
