Federal elections decide who sits in the House of Commons. The citizenship test expects the mechanics, not campaign slogans.

## Who may vote

You may vote in a federal election if you are:

- A **Canadian citizen**
- At least **18** on election day
- On the voters’ list (you can also register at the polling station in many cases)

Elections Canada runs federal elections. It is independent of the government of the day.

Permanent residents cannot vote federally, however long they have lived here. That is one of the practical differences citizenship makes.

## Getting on the voters’ list

Elections Canada keeps a permanent register of electors. If you are on it, a voter information card arrives in the mail telling you where and when to vote. If you are not, you can register online, or at the polling station when you show up.

Historically, **enumeration** meant officials going door to door before each election to build the list by hand. The permanent register replaced most of that work.

If you **move**, update your registration. Your vote belongs to the riding where you live, so an out-of-date address can send you to the wrong poll.

## Ridings and first past the post

The country is divided into **electoral districts**, also called **ridings**. Each riding elects **one MP**. The candidate with the most votes in that riding wins—even without 50%. This is **single-member plurality**, often called first past the post.

The party that can command a majority of MPs usually forms the government. If no party has a majority of seats, a **minority government** may govern with support from others. If two or more parties formally agreed to govern together, that would be a **coalition**—rare in federal Canadian practice.

Two consequences of counting seats rather than national votes are worth knowing:

- Winning the nationwide **popular vote** does not automatically make a party the government. Seats decide.
- **Vote splitting** happens when similar parties divide support and let another candidate take the riding with well under half the votes.

Ridings are redrawn periodically because populations shift. This **reapportionment** adjusts how many Commons seats each province holds, under the Constitution and federal statute, and independent commissions draw the boundaries. The aim is roughly comparable numbers of people per MP, with allowances for northern and rural districts where strict equality would make ridings unmanageably large.

## How an election is called

An election formally begins when **writs of election** are issued—the official orders that start the contest in each riding. A campaign has a minimum length set by law, so there is always time to nominate candidates and organize the vote.

In each riding, a **returning officer** is the local Elections Canada official responsible for running everything: hiring staff, setting up polls, and reporting results.

## Ways to vote

Election day is not the only option:

- **Advance polls** open before election day for people who cannot vote easily on the day itself.
- **Special ballots**, including mail-in voting, exist for electors who cannot get to an ordinary poll—travelling, living abroad, in hospital, or in the armed forces.

To vote, you generally have to **prove your identity and address** under Elections Canada's rules. There is more than one accepted combination of documents, and a voter who lacks them can in some cases have another elector vouch for them. Note that identification proves who you are; it does not create eligibility. Someone who is not a citizen cannot vote by producing a utility bill.

## Secret ballot and fairness

The **secret ballot** means no one should see how you voted. Treating, intimidation, and impersonation are crimes. Advertising and spending are regulated. There are limits on how long a Parliament may last; an election must be called within that limit, and governments often go to the people sooner.

Ballot secrecy is a rule about the polling place, not only a personal choice, which is why photographing your marked ballot to show others is not permitted. Spending limits apply to parties and candidates, and **third-party advertising** by other groups is regulated too, so no one can spend without limits or disclosure.

By-elections fill vacancies when an MP resigns or dies.

## Who is in the room on election day

- **Deputy returning officers** and **poll clerks** administer the vote and keep the records. Their job is to run the poll fairly, not to campaign.
- **Scrutineers** are representatives of parties or candidates who may watch the process and the count. They are observers, not officials.

Ballots are counted by hand at the poll once voting closes. When a margin is very close, or there are serious concerns about the count, a **recount** may be requested or required—in some circumstances conducted by a judge.

## Running for office

Citizens may seek nomination and run for Parliament, subject to rules on deposits, signatures, and eligibility. You do not have to belong to a party, but most successful candidates do. A candidate who runs without a party label is an **independent**.

Parties campaign on a **platform**—the promises and policies they offer voters. You do not need to be a candidate to take part: anyone may volunteer on a campaign, and non-citizens can volunteer even though they cannot vote.

## After the vote

- MPs take their seats in the Commons
- The Governor General invites a leader to form a government
- Cabinet is sworn in
- The Speech from the Throne sets out the government’s program

Voting is a right and a responsibility. Nobody can legally force you to say which party you support.
