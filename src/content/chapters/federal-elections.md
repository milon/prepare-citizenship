Federal elections decide who sits in the House of Commons. The citizenship test expects the mechanics, not campaign slogans.

## Who may vote

You may vote in a federal election if you are:

- A **Canadian citizen**
- At least **18** on election day
- On the voters’ list (you can also register at the polling station in many cases)

Elections Canada runs federal elections. It is independent of the government of the day.

## Ridings and first past the post

The country is divided into **electoral districts**, also called **ridings**. Each riding elects **one MP**. The candidate with the most votes in that riding wins—even without 50%. This is **single-member plurality**, often called first past the post.

The party that can command a majority of MPs usually forms the government. If no party has a majority of seats, a **minority government** may govern with support from others.

## Secret ballot and fairness

The **secret ballot** means no one should see how you voted. Treating, intimidation, and impersonation are crimes. Advertising and spending are regulated. There are limits on how long a Parliament may last; an election must be called within that limit, and governments often go to the people sooner.

By-elections fill vacancies when an MP resigns or dies.

## Running for office

Citizens may seek nomination and run for Parliament, subject to rules on deposits, signatures, and eligibility. You do not have to belong to a party, but most successful candidates do.

## After the vote

- MPs take their seats in the Commons
- The Governor General invites a leader to form a government
- Cabinet is sworn in
- The Speech from the Throne sets out the government’s program

Voting is a right and a responsibility. Nobody can legally force you to say which party you support.
