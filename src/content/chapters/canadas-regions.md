Canada is the world’s second-largest country by area, with most people living within a few hundred kilometres of the United States border. For study purposes the official guide groups the country into five regions.

## The political map

There are **ten provinces** and **three territories**. Provinces have constitutional powers of their own. Territories were created by federal statute and have been gaining more self-government over time.

The **national capital** is **Ottawa**. Do not confuse it with Toronto (Ontario’s capital and the largest city) or with provincial capitals in general.

## Five regions

| Region | Jurisdictions |
| --- | --- |
| Atlantic | Newfoundland and Labrador, Prince Edward Island, Nova Scotia, New Brunswick |
| Central | Quebec, Ontario |
| Prairie | Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta |
| West Coast | British Columbia |
| North | Yukon, Northwest Territories, Nunavut |

## Physical geography worth remembering

- The **Canadian Shield** is a vast, rocky, mineral-rich expanse around Hudson Bay
- The **Great Lakes** (Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, Ontario) drain to the Atlantic via the St. Lawrence
- The **Prairies** are agricultural plains
- The **Cordillera** is the western mountain system
- The **Arctic** is sparsely populated, strategically important, and home to Inuit and other northern peoples

## How to study this chapter

National facts first (capital, number of provinces and territories, the five-region grouping). Then memorize **your** capital city, a neighbour or two, and one economic or historic hook. Regional questions on the real test often target the applicant’s province.
