The three territories cover an enormous share of Canada’s landmass and a small share of its people. They are not provinces. Ottawa retains a larger formal role, though elected territorial governments now run most day-to-day services.

## Yukon

- Capital: **Whitehorse**
- The **Klondike Gold Rush** (late 1890s) brought a rush of prospectors and made the territory famous
- Relatively connected by road compared with Nunavut

## Northwest Territories

- Capital: **Yellowknife**, on **Great Slave Lake**
- A large territory with many Indigenous languages; several of those languages have official status alongside English and French
- Several regions have land-claim and self-government agreements
- Diamonds, gold, and public administration are economic pillars

## Nunavut

- Created in **1999** from the eastern Northwest Territories
- Capital: **Iqaluit**
- Majority **Inuit** population; Inuktut languages are central to public life
- Communities are mostly coastal and fly-in; cost of living is high

## Shared northern facts

Permafrost, long winter darkness and summer light, Arctic sovereignty, and climate change are part of how the North is discussed in national politics. For the test, lock in the three capitals, the 1999 creation of Nunavut, and the Klondike as Yukon’s historic hook.
