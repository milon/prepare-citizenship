The Prairie provinces sit between Ontario’s Shield and the Rockies. They joined Canada after 1867: Manitoba in 1870, then the two newer provinces in 1905.

## Manitoba

- Capital: **Winnipeg**, at the fork of the Red and Assiniboine rivers
- **Red River** Métis history, including Louis Riel and the 1870 entry into Confederation
- Large Indigenous population relative to some other provinces
- Economy: agriculture, transport, hydroelectricity, and services

## Saskatchewan

- Capital: **Regina**
- **Saskatoon** is the largest city
- Known for **wheat** and other grains, and for **potash**
- Birthplace of a major strain of Canadian social democracy, including early public hospital insurance

## Alberta

- Capital: **Edmonton**
- Largest city: **Calgary**
- Energy (oil and gas) is central to the provincial economy; agriculture remains important
- The Rocky Mountains along the British Columbia boundary are a tourism and conservation landmark

## Shared Prairie themes

Settlement at the turn of the twentieth century drew people from Eastern Europe, including large Ukrainian communities, as well as from Ontario, the United States, and elsewhere. First Nations and Métis were already there; numbered treaties cover much of the region. Cold winters, open land, and export agriculture still shape how people describe the Prairies—even in oil-rich Alberta.
