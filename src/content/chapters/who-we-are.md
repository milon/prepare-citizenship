Canada is a bilingual, multicultural country on Indigenous lands. The citizenship test expects you to know who was here first, how French and British institutions took shape, and how later immigration changed the country.

## Indigenous peoples

Three groups are recognized as **Indigenous peoples**:

- **First Nations** — many distinct nations with their own languages and governments, living in every region.
- **Inuit** — the Indigenous people of the Arctic, whose homeland is often called Inuit Nunangat.
- **Métis** — a people of mixed First Nations and European ancestry who formed a distinct culture, especially on the Prairies.

Indigenous peoples have constitutional rights. Treaties, historic and modern, set out relationships with the Crown. Knowing this history—including residential schools and other injustices—is part of being an informed citizen, even where the official study guide is brief.

## French and English Canada

French settlement in the St. Lawrence valley created a Catholic, French-speaking society that survived the British Conquest of 1759–1763. The **Quebec Act (1774)** let that community keep its faith, language, and civil law.

English-speaking Canada grew from British colonies, Loyalists who left the United States, and later immigration from the British Isles and beyond. **English and French** are the official languages of Parliament and the federal government.

**Acadians** are descendants of early French settlers in the Maritimes. After the deportation of the 1750s, many returned; Acadian communities remain, especially in New Brunswick.

## Immigration and regional communities

Canada is often described as a land of immigrants. Large communities with roots in Europe, Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and Latin America live in every region, with especially large immigrant populations in cities such as Toronto, Montreal, and Vancouver.

Some groups the study guide highlights because they shaped particular regions:

- **Ukrainian** settlers on the Prairies
- **Chinese** workers who helped build the railway, later facing a head tax
- **Sikh** and other South Asian communities, especially in British Columbia
- **Jewish**, **Italian**, **Portuguese**, **Greek**, and many other communities in central Canadian cities

## Canadian identity

There is no single cultural test for being Canadian. Shared institutions—parliamentary government, the rule of law, official bilingualism at the federal level, and equality of women and men—are the civic core. Regional identities (Quebec, the Atlantic, the West, the North) are real and expected knowledge for the test.
